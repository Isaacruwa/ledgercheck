The Good Faith Estimate Dispute Process: How the $400 Rule Actually Works

EOBCheck · Updated August 25, 2026 · 7 min read

If you're uninsured or paying out of pocket, you're generally entitled to a written cost estimate before a scheduled medical service. If your final bill comes in significantly higher, federal rules give you a real, formal way to challenge it. Here's how that actually works.

What a Good Faith Estimate actually is

A Good Faith Estimate (GFE) is a written estimate of expected charges for a scheduled service, given to uninsured or self-pay patients before the service happens. It's meant to prevent exactly the situation this article is about — a final bill that bears no resemblance to what you were told to expect.

The $400 rule

If your final bill ends up $400 or more above your Good Faith Estimate, from a single provider or facility, you may be eligible to dispute that charge through a federal process rather than just negotiating informally.

Important nuance: this threshold is calculated per provider or facility, not combined across your whole treatment. If three different providers each billed you $300 over their own individual estimates, none of them alone crosses $400 — even though your combined overage is $900. This is one of the most commonly misunderstood parts of the rule.

Who this applies to

  • You're uninsured, or you're not using your insurance for this particular service (self-pay).
  • You received a written Good Faith Estimate before the service.
  • Your final bill from that same provider is $400+ above that estimate.

Eligibility details and filing windows can shift over time, so treat this as a starting point for your own research, not a final legal determination — verify your specific situation before assuming you qualify or don't.

How to actually file a dispute

  1. Gather your Good Faith Estimate and your final, itemized bill.
  2. Confirm the gap is $400 or more from that one provider specifically.
  3. Look up the current federal Patient-Provider Dispute Resolution process — this is administered through HHS and has its own filing window after you receive the bill, so don't sit on this for months.
  4. Submit your estimate, your bill, and a short explanation of the discrepancy.
Before you file: it's worth first asking the provider directly for a written explanation of the gap. Sometimes what looks like a GFE violation is actually an added service you didn't originally expect (which may or may not still qualify), and a quick conversation can save you the formal filing entirely — or make your dispute stronger if you do file.

What this process can and can't do

A successful dispute can result in your bill being reduced to the estimate amount (or close to it), or a mediated settlement. It won't erase a bill that was accurate and simply higher than you personally expected without a GFE ever being issued — the whole process is anchored to that written estimate existing in the first place.
